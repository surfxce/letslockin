[00:00] Right, so today we're finishing off gene regulation in prokaryotes, and I want
to spend most of the hour on the lac operon because it's the model everything else gets
compared to. Make sure you know this one properly.

[01:20] So E. coli would rather eat glucose than lactose. Given both, it eats the glucose
first, and only when the glucose runs out does it start making the enzymes to handle
lactose. That's the behaviour we're explaining.

[03:05] The operon itself is three structural genes — lacZ, lacY, lacA — transcribed as
one polycistronic message. lacZ is beta-galactosidase, which cleaves lactose into glucose
and galactose. lacY is the permease, which is the transporter that gets lactose into the
cell in the first place. lacA is a transacetylase and honestly you can more or less
ignore it for the exam.

[06:40] Upstream you've got the promoter and the operator. The lacI gene, which sits
outside the operon, is constitutively expressed and makes the repressor protein. The
repressor binds the operator and physically blocks RNA polymerase from transcribing.
That's negative control — the default state is off.

[09:15] Now here's the bit people get wrong every year. The inducer is not lactose. It's
allolactose, which is an isomer of lactose produced as a side reaction by
beta-galactosidase. Allolactose binds the repressor, changes its conformation, and the
repressor lets go of the operator. So you need a little bit of beta-galactosidase around
already for induction to get going, which is why there's always a trickle of basal
expression.

[13:50] Second layer, and this is the positive control. cAMP levels are inversely related
to glucose. High glucose, low cAMP. Low glucose, high cAMP. cAMP binds CAP — catabolite
activator protein, sometimes called CRP — and the cAMP-CAP complex binds just upstream of
the promoter and recruits RNA polymerase. The lac promoter is genuinely weak on its own,
so without CAP you get very little transcription even with the repressor gone.

[18:30] So put those together and you get four states. Glucose present, lactose absent:
repressor on, no CAP, off. Glucose present, lactose present: repressor off but no CAP, so
you get a trickle, not full expression. Glucose absent, lactose absent: CAP bound but
repressor still on, off. Glucose absent, lactose present: repressor off, CAP on, full
expression. That four-way table is examinable, I'd learn it.

[24:10] Quick contrast with the trp operon since it comes up. Trp is repressible rather
than inducible — the default is on, and tryptophan acts as a corepressor that activates
the repressor. So lac is an inducible catabolic operon, trp is a repressible anabolic one.
Same logic, opposite sign.
